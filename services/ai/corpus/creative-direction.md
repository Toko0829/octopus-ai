# Creative direction for paid campaigns

## Creative is the main lever, so treat it as the main work

On platforms with strong automated targeting, the system finds the audience and
the creative decides whether the campaign works. Effort spent refining audience
settings has a low ceiling. Effort spent producing genuinely different creative
does not.

This reverses the intuitive allocation. Most of the time budgeted to a campaign
should go into making things to test, not into configuring the account.

## The first moment carries the result

Attention is decided before the message is delivered. In video that is the
opening seconds; in static it is whatever is legible at a glance while
scrolling. If the opening does not select for the right person, the rest of the
asset is never seen and its quality is irrelevant.

A useful test: if the opening would work equally well for a competitor's
product, it is not selecting anyone.

## Distinguish concept from execution

A concept is the underlying claim or angle: who this is for, what changes, why
now. An execution is how that concept is rendered: the format, the visuals, the
wording.

Running five executions of one concept and calling it a test is the most common
waste in paid social. It measures rendering, not ideas, and produces small
differences that do not survive scale. Test concepts against each other first,
then refine execution within whichever concept wins.

## What a usable brief contains

A brief that a person or a generation tool can act on states:

The audience and the moment they are in.

The single claim the asset must land.

The proof for that claim, since an unsupported claim is both weaker and riskier.

The format and where it will run, because the same idea is built differently for
different placements.

The constraint, meaning what must not be said. This is where brand-safety and
disclosure obligations enter the creative process rather than being checked
after the fact.

Briefs missing the claim produce assets that look finished and say nothing.

## Polish is not the same as performance

Highly produced assets frequently lose to plainer ones that look native to the
surface they appear on. Production value signals advertising, and audiences
discount advertising. This is not an argument for carelessness; it is an
argument that the money belongs in the idea rather than in the finish.

## Creative fatigues, and the numbers show it first

Performance decay on a stable audience usually means the asset has been seen too
often rather than that the offer stopped working. The response is new concepts,
not budget changes. Treat a library of concepts as inventory to be replenished
on a schedule.

## When to bring in a human

Taste, brand direction, and high-end editing are the clearest cases for an
expert. So is the situation where several honest concept tests have all
underperformed: that is usually a positioning problem surfacing as a creative
problem, and more assets will not resolve it.

## The brief, written out

A brief is one page and it is written before anything is made. The sections
below are the ones whose absence shows up in the finished asset.

**The audience and the moment.** Not a demographic. The situation the person is
in when the asset reaches them, and what they were doing a second before.

**The single claim.** One sentence, stating what becomes true for that person.
If two claims are listed, the asset will make neither, because an asset has a few
seconds and cannot spend them twice.

**The proof.** What makes the claim believable: a number, a demonstration, a
named use case, a quote. A claim without proof is both weaker and riskier,
because unsupported claims are also the part of a campaign most likely to attract
a policy decision.

**The format and placement.** The same idea is built differently for a feed than
for a search result, and a brief that does not say where it runs produces an
asset that fits nowhere.

**The constraint.** What must not be said, and what must be included. This is
where disclosure obligations and brand-safety rules enter the process, before the
asset exists rather than after it has been approved by three people.

**The reference.** One or two examples of the register you want, with a sentence
on what specifically about them is the point. Without this, "make it feel
premium" is interpreted five different ways.

A brief missing the claim is the common failure. It produces assets that look
finished and say nothing, and the review conversation is about colour because
there is nothing else to discuss.

## Hooks: the shapes that recur

A hook is the first moment, and it is the part of the asset that decides whether
the rest is seen. Most hooks that work are one of a small number of shapes, which
is useful because it turns "think of an idea" into "try each of these".

**The problem, stated in the audience's own words.** Selects precisely, because
the people without the problem keep scrolling and the people with it stop dead.

**The unexpected claim.** Something that contradicts what the audience assumes.
It has to be true and it has to be defended immediately, or it reads as bait.

**The demonstration.** The product doing the thing, without preamble. Strongest
where the value is visible and weakest where it is not.

**The before and after.** Concrete, specific, and dated where possible.

**The objection, said first.** Naming the reason someone would not buy, before
they think of it, buys enough credibility to make the rest hearable.

**The comparison to the manual way.** Effective because the real competitor is
usually a workaround rather than a rival product.

The test on any of them is whether it would work equally well for a competitor.
If it would, it is not selecting anyone, and it will buy expensive clicks from
people who were never going to convert.

## What counts as a different concept

A test of concepts is only a test if the concepts differ. In practice most tests
labelled as concept tests are execution tests, and they produce small differences
that vanish at scale.

Two assets are different concepts when they make different claims, or aim at
different moments, or offer different proof. They are the same concept when they
differ in colour, length, music, voice, layout, or the order of the same points.

The practical rule: write each asset's claim in one sentence. If the sentences
are the same, it is one concept.

Execution tests are worth running, but only after a concept has won, and only
when there is enough volume to detect a small difference. At early spend there
almost never is.

## Iterating on a concept that half-worked

A concept that is close to the target is usually worth three more attempts, and
the attempts should vary specific things rather than being generally better.

If the hook earned attention and the asset lost people immediately after, the
opening is writing a cheque the middle does not honour. Keep the hook, replace
what follows.

If people watched or read to the end and did not act, the claim landed and the
ask did not follow from it. The call to action is usually either absent, vague,
or asking for something larger than the asset earned.

If nobody stopped at all, the hook is the problem and everything after it is
untested. Replace the first moment and change nothing else.

Naming which of the three happened is the whole value of watching the asset's
own numbers, and it is the difference between iterating and redoing.

## Volume, and where it comes from

A campaign needs a supply of concepts, not a supply of assets, and concepts are
the scarcer thing. The cheapest sources of them are all outside the design tool.

Sales conversations, where the objection that recurs is a hook. Support requests,
where the confusion that recurs is a claim. Customer reviews and competitor
reviews, which are written in the audience's vocabulary. The founder's own
reasoning about why the product exists, which is usually more distinctive than
anything written afterwards.

Keep them in a list as they arrive. The scarce resource in a paid programme is
not production capacity, it is ideas worth producing, and they are collected
rather than invented on the day the campaign needs them.

## Plainness, deliberately

Assets that look native to the surface they appear on generally outperform highly
produced ones, because production value signals advertising and audiences
discount advertising.

This is not permission to be careless. The plain asset still has to be legible at
a glance, still has to make one claim, still has to be well lit and well written.
What it does not need is the polish that announces a budget.

The practical version: spend the money on the idea and on the person delivering
it, and spend the remaining time on making more concepts rather than on finishing
one to a higher standard.
