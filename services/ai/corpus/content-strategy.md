# Content strategy when nobody knows you yet

## Content has two different jobs, and they need different pieces

Content made for **search** answers a question someone already typed. It can be
found months after publication and compounds slowly.

Content made for **distribution** interrupts someone who was not looking. It
succeeds or fails within a day or two and does not compound.

A single piece rarely does both well, because the openings differ: search
content should answer immediately, distribution content has to earn attention
before it is allowed to answer. Deciding which job a piece has, before writing
it, prevents the common outcome of publishing something that ranks for nothing
and is shared by nobody.

## One idea per piece

The instinct with limited output is to make each piece comprehensive. It reads
as thorough and performs poorly, because a reader cannot repeat it to anyone.
Pieces that travel tend to make one argument that can be summarised in a
sentence. Splitting a broad piece into three narrow ones usually produces more
total reach than the original.

## Publish from the problem, not from the product

Content built around product features reaches people who already know the
product exists, which at this stage is almost nobody. Content built around the
problem reaches people experiencing it, which is a much larger group and the one
worth converting.

The product earns a mention once the piece has genuinely helped. Leading with it
converts the few readers who arrived ready to buy and loses the rest.

## Depth beats cadence

A publishing schedule is a proxy metric. It is easy to measure and easy to hit
by lowering quality, and shallow output accumulates maintenance cost without
accumulating authority. Fewer, better pieces outperform, and they age better.

If cadence is used at all, it should be the maximum sustainable rate at the
quality bar, not a target that quality bends to meet.

## Repurpose deliberately, not mechanically

One substantial piece can supply a month of shorter derivatives, but copying the
same text across surfaces performs badly because each surface has its own norms.
Repurposing works when the _idea_ is carried across and the _execution_ is
rebuilt for the destination.

## Measure the leading indicator, not the vanity one

Views measure distribution, not interest. The signals worth watching are the
ones closest to intent: how many readers reached the end, how many took the next
step offered, and how many joined something you own, such as an email list.

Those are the numbers to count per piece: subscribers gained, signups started,
enquiries received. Attributing them to the piece that produced them is what
turns a content plan from a schedule into a decision about what to write next.

A piece with modest traffic and a high next-step rate is more valuable than a
popular piece that converts nobody, and it tells you what to write next.

## When to bring in a human

Voice-critical work, where the writing is the product's personality, is worth a
human writer earlier than most founders expect. Generated drafts are adequate at
explaining and weak at sounding like someone in particular.

## Choosing what to write about

The hardest part of a content plan is not the writing, and a plan that starts
from a calendar rather than from a list of questions produces filler.

Start from questions the buyer already asks. Sales calls, support requests,
onboarding confusion, and the message people send before they buy are all lists
of topics written by the audience. A question asked three times by strangers is a
better topic than anything generated from a keyword tool, because it is evidence
of demand rather than an estimate of it.

Then add the arguments only you can make. A founder has a position on why the
problem exists and why the usual approach fails, and that position is not
available to competitors. It is also the material most likely to be shared,
because agreement and disagreement both travel.

Then add the practical pieces: how to do the thing the product automates, done
manually. These convert unusually well, because the reader who follows them
experiences the problem the product solves while reading, and they rank, because
somebody typed the question.

What to leave out: company news, feature announcements aimed at people who do not
know the company exists, and topics chosen because a competitor covered them.

Rank the list by whether you can say something specific. A topic where the honest
draft would be generic is a topic to skip, whatever its estimated volume.

## Deciding the job of each piece before writing it

Every piece is either search-shaped or distribution-shaped, and the decision
changes the opening, the length, and where it goes afterwards.

A search piece answers the question in the first paragraph, then earns the rest.
Its opening assumes the reader arrived deliberately and is impatient. It should
be updated rather than replaced when it goes stale, because its value accrues.

A distribution piece earns attention before it is allowed to answer. Its opening
assumes the reader was doing something else. It has a short life and should be
written to be finished in one sitting.

Attempting both produces a piece that opens too slowly to rank and answers too
quickly to be shared. Writing the intended job at the top of the draft, for
yourself, is enough to prevent it.

## Formats, chosen by what the idea needs

The written piece is the default because it is searchable, quotable and cheap to
revise. Everything else should have a reason.

A demonstration is right when the value is visible and hard to describe. A
teardown or worked example is right when the audience learns by watching somebody
competent do the thing. An interview is right when the credibility you need is
somebody else's. A short-form piece is right when the idea is one sentence and
the goal is reach.

Length is decided by the idea, not by a standard. A thorough piece that runs long
because the question is genuinely involved will outperform, and a piece padded to
a word count reads exactly like one.

## Distribution is half the work and is usually unplanned

Publishing is not distributing, and a plan that ends at publish reliably
underdelivers.

Decide before writing where the piece will be seen. The realistic options for a
small product are an owned list, the founder's own social presence, communities
where the question is genuinely being asked, and people who already write about
the topic and may reference it.

Sending it to the list is the highest-yield step and the one most often skipped
out of a worry about mailing too often. A list that never hears from you is not
being protected, it is decaying.

Posting into a community requires having been there first. Arriving to link is
recognised instantly and costs the account.

Reaching out to people who cover the topic works when the piece genuinely adds
something they did not have. It does not work as a favour request.

Plan for a piece to be distributed more than once. Publishing it, sharing it,
then referencing it again weeks later when it is relevant to something reaches
three different sets of people.

## Repurposing that works

The rule is that the idea travels and the execution is rebuilt. Copying the text
across surfaces performs badly on all of them.

A substantial piece typically yields: several short posts each making one of its
points, one of which is usually stronger standalone than it was inside the piece;
an email that makes the argument in the reader's inbox rather than linking away;
a demonstration or visual of the one part that is hard to describe in words; and
a section that can be lifted into a landing page as proof.

Doing that deliberately is the difference between one piece a month and a month
of material.

## Measuring a piece

Views measure distribution. The numbers worth recording per piece are the ones
closest to intent: how many readers reached the end, how many joined something
you own, and how many took the next step offered.

Record them per piece rather than in aggregate, because the aggregate cannot
answer the only question a content plan has, which is what to write next.

Give every piece a next step. A piece with no next step converts none of its
readers, and it is the most common reason a content programme reports traffic and
produces nothing.

Read the results over months rather than weeks, because search content compounds
slowly and a piece judged at two weeks is judged before it has done its job.

## When to stop

A content programme that has run for months and produced no subscribers,
enquiries or signups is not early, it is not working, and continuing at the same
cadence is the expensive option.

The diagnosis is usually one of three. The topics attract readers who were never
going to buy, which is a positioning problem showing up in the choice of subject.
The pieces have no next step, which is fixable in an afternoon. Or the pieces are
generic, in which case more of them will not help.

Stopping to write three genuinely good pieces is almost always better than
continuing to publish weekly at a standard nobody has a reason to read.
