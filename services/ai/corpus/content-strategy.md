# Content strategy when nobody knows you yet

## Content has two different jobs, and they need different pieces

Content made for **search** answers a question someone already typed. It can be
found months after publication and compounds slowly.

Content made for **distribution** interrupts someone who was not looking. It
succeeds or fails within a day or two and does not compound.

A single piece rarely does both well, because the openings differ: search
content should answer immediately, distribution content has to earn attention
before it is allowed to answer. Deciding which job a piece has, before writing
it, prevents the common outcome of publishing something that ranks for nothing
and is shared by nobody.

## One idea per piece

The instinct with limited output is to make each piece comprehensive. It reads
as thorough and performs poorly, because a reader cannot repeat it to anyone.
Pieces that travel tend to make one argument that can be summarised in a
sentence. Splitting a broad piece into three narrow ones usually produces more
total reach than the original.

## Publish from the problem, not from the product

Content built around product features reaches people who already know the
product exists, which at this stage is almost nobody. Content built around the
problem reaches people experiencing it, which is a much larger group and the one
worth converting.

The product earns a mention once the piece has genuinely helped. Leading with it
converts the few readers who arrived ready to buy and loses the rest.

## Depth beats cadence

A publishing schedule is a proxy metric. It is easy to measure and easy to hit
by lowering quality, and shallow output accumulates maintenance cost without
accumulating authority. Fewer, better pieces outperform, and they age better.

If cadence is used at all, it should be the maximum sustainable rate at the
quality bar, not a target that quality bends to meet.

## Repurpose deliberately, not mechanically

One substantial piece can supply a month of shorter derivatives, but copying the
same text across surfaces performs badly because each surface has its own norms.
Repurposing works when the _idea_ is carried across and the _execution_ is
rebuilt for the destination.

## Measure the leading indicator, not the vanity one

Views measure distribution, not interest. The signals worth watching are the
ones closest to intent: how many readers reached the end, how many took the next
step offered, and how many joined something you own, such as an email list.

Those are the numbers to count per piece: subscribers gained, signups started,
enquiries received. Attributing them to the piece that produced them is what
turns a content plan from a schedule into a decision about what to write next.

A piece with modest traffic and a high next-step rate is more valuable than a
popular piece that converts nobody, and it tells you what to write next.

## When to bring in a human

Voice-critical work, where the writing is the product's personality, is worth a
human writer earlier than most founders expect. Generated drafts are adequate at
explaining and weak at sounding like someone in particular.
