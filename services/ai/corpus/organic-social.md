# Organic social for a founder-led product

## Treat it as distribution, not as brand presence

Maintaining a profile because a business is expected to have one produces
posting without purpose and costs more time than it returns. Organic social
earns its place when it is doing a specific job: putting the product's ideas in
front of people who do not know it exists.

If a channel is not doing that job, the honest options are to change how it is
used or to stop, not to keep posting at a lower standard.

## The founder has an advantage the brand account does not

Audiences engage with people more readily than with companies, and platforms
tend to reflect that. For a solo founder this is a structural advantage rather
than a preference: building in public, showing the reasoning behind decisions,
and posting from a personal account usually outperforms the same material
published by a logo.

It also carries a real cost worth naming. Attention accrues to the person, not
the asset, and it is not transferable if the founder later steps back.

## Native beats cross-posted

Every surface has its own norms for length, pacing, and what an opening looks
like. Publishing one asset identically everywhere reads as syndicated and
performs worse on all of them than a version rebuilt for each. The idea travels
between platforms; the execution should not.

## Consistency compounds, virality does not

A post that reaches far once produces a spike and little durable change. Regular
posting at a standard the audience can rely on produces a slower curve that
keeps rising. Planning around the possibility of going viral is planning around
an outcome that cannot be commissioned.

The practical implication is to choose a cadence sustainable in a bad week
rather than a good one.

## Replies are distribution

Conversation in comments and replies is typically weighted heavily by these
platforms, and it reaches the audiences of the people being replied to. Time
spent in other people's threads often produces more reach for a new account than
time spent producing another post of one's own.

## Follower count is the least useful number available

It measures accumulated history, not current reach, and it can grow while
influence falls. The signals worth tracking are how many people saw the material
who do not already follow, how many took the offered next step, and how many
moved to a channel you control.

An account with a few thousand followers that produces subscribers, enquiries and
paying customers every week is worth more than one with ten times the following
and no next step. Follower count is the number other people judge an account by,
which is not the same as the number worth managing it against.

## Move attention to something you own

Social audiences are rented. Reach depends on ranking decisions that change
without notice, and an account can lose distribution or be suspended without
recourse. The purpose of the channel is partly to convert borrowed attention
into an owned one, which in practice usually means an email list.

A post that earns wide reach and offers no next step converts none of it, and
the reach does not return.

## What to post, when there is nothing to announce

The blank-page problem is the reason most founder accounts stop, and it comes
from believing a post has to be news. Almost none of the posts that work are
news.

The reliable material is the reasoning behind decisions. Why the product works
the way it does, what was tried first and abandoned, what the common approach
gets wrong. This is available every week, it is specific to you, and it is the
material that makes strangers trust the product before they have used it.

Next is the work itself, shown. A problem being solved, a before and after, a
number that moved and why. Specific beats impressive: a small real result is more
persuasive than a large vague one.

Next is answering a question publicly that somebody asked privately. The question
is evidence that others have it, and the answer is a post that was already
written.

Next is a position, argued. Agreement and disagreement both distribute, and a
post nobody could disagree with is a post nobody repeats.

What consistently fails: announcements about the company, motivational material
with no subject, and reposted content from elsewhere with a comment attached.

## The mechanics that apply everywhere

Platforms differ and their norms change, so the durable rules are the ones about
how attention works rather than about any surface.

The first line is the whole decision. It is what is shown before anyone chooses
to expand, and if it does not select the right reader nothing after it is read.
Write it last, from the strongest sentence in the post.

One idea per post, for the same reason one idea per content piece: a post
carrying three points cannot be repeated by anybody.

Post the substance in the post rather than linking away. Surfaces suppress
material that sends people elsewhere, and readers do not follow links from
accounts they do not know. Where a link is necessary, earn it inside the post
first.

Be present for the first stretch after posting. Replies in the early window do
more for reach than anything about the post itself, and they are also where the
actual conversations start.

## Cadence, decided honestly

Choose the rate sustainable in a bad week, not a good one. A schedule that
survives a launch, a holiday and an illness is a schedule that compounds; one
that breaks produces a month of silence followed by an apology post.

For a founder account, a small number of considered posts a week outperforms
daily filler, because the account is being judged on whether it is worth reading
rather than on frequency.

Batch the writing and space the publishing. The scarce resource is thinking
time, and it does not arrive daily.

Consistency matters more than the specific number, because it is what makes the
account something people expect rather than something they encounter.

## Replies and other people's threads

For a new account, time spent in other people's conversations usually produces
more reach than another post, because it borrows an audience that already exists
and it puts the reply in front of people who are demonstrably interested in the
topic.

The rule is to add something. A reply that agrees adds nothing and is read as
promotion. A reply that supplies a specific piece of experience, a
counterexample, or the part the original left out is read as expertise and is
what makes people look at the profile.

Choose the threads where you actually know something. The strategy fails when it
becomes commenting on everything, which is visible and costs credibility faster
than it earns reach.

## Converting attention into something you own

This is the whole point of the channel, and it is where most founder accounts
fail, because they earn attention and never ask for anything.

Have one destination and one reason to go there. Not a homepage: something with a
specific promise, which is the thing the audience gets by giving an email
address. A post that earns wide reach and offers no next step converts none of
it, and the reach does not come back.

Ask in the post that earned attention rather than only in the profile. The
profile is read by a small fraction of the people who read the post.

Ask in proportion to what was given. A post that genuinely helped can ask;
a post that did nothing cannot, and asking anyway is what trains people to
scroll past the account.

Where the platform makes links expensive, the reply is the place for it, or the
profile with a post that points at the profile.

## Reading the numbers

Follower count measures accumulated history and can rise while influence falls.
The numbers worth watching, in order:

How many of the people who saw a post do not already follow. This is whether the
account is reaching outward or talking to itself.

How many took the next step offered, per post. This is what identifies which
ideas convert, which is the finding that transfers to ads, pages and email.

How many joined something you own this week. This is the only number that
survives the platform changing its mind.

Read them per post and over months. A single post reaching far tells you
nothing repeatable; a pattern in which kinds of posts produce subscribers tells
you what to write.

## When to stop, or change

An account that has posted consistently for months at a decent standard and
produced no subscribers, enquiries or customers is doing a different job than
intended, and the honest options are to change how it is used or to stop.

The usual causes are that the posts have no next step, that the material is
about the company rather than about the reader's problem, or that the audience
being reached is not the audience that buys. The last of those is a positioning
finding rather than a social one, and it is worth more than the channel.

Stopping is a legitimate outcome. A channel maintained at a low standard because
a business is expected to have one costs founder time, which is the scarcest
input in the plan.
