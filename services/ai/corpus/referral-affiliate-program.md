# Referral and affiliate programs

## Two different programs that get one name

A referral program asks existing customers to bring people they already know. An
affiliate program pays partners to promote you to audiences you do not have. They
are discussed together and behave nothing alike.

Referrals are motivated by the relationship. The referrer's reputation is on the
line, so volume is small, quality is high, and the reward can be modest because it
is not really why they did it. Affiliates are motivated by the commission: volume
can be far larger, quality varies enormously, and the incentive is the whole
mechanism. Deciding which you are building comes first, because a program
designed as both usually works as neither.

## A program amplifies demand, it does not create it

The common failure is launching one to fix a growth problem it cannot fix. If
customers are not already recommending the product unprompted, an incentive will
not start them, it will only pay the few who would have anyway. If new
subscribers churn quickly, affiliates are being paid to fill a leaking bucket, and
the faster they work the more it costs.

The prerequisites are unglamorous: something people are glad they bought, a
retention curve that flattens rather than falling to nothing, and a moment where
somebody is pleased enough to tell a friend. That moment is also where the ask
belongs, rather than in a footer.

## Structuring the incentive

For referrals, reward both sides. A two-sided reward lets the referrer offer
their friend something rather than extract something from them, which turns the
share from a sales act into a gift, and it is the largest single determinant of
whether anybody uses it.

For affiliates, four decisions define the program. Percentage of sale or flat
amount per customer: percentages suit variable prices, flat amounts are easier to
promote and forecast. One-off or recurring: recurring commission attracts serious
partners and commits you to a permanent margin reduction, so model it over the
customer's expected lifetime rather than the first payment. How long a referred
visitor stays credited, which trades fairness to the partner against paying twice
for one customer. And what the commission comes out of, which must be gross
margin rather than revenue, or a generous-looking rate quietly sells at a loss.

Anchor the rate to what a customer is worth and what the margin carries, then
check it against comparable products, because partners compare. A rate you cannot
sustain is worse than a modest one, since cutting it later costs you the partners
it attracted.

## Recruiting the first partners

Your best customers are the first affiliates worth asking: they describe the
product accurately and already believe it. After them, the people already talking
about the problem you solve. A program page nobody is sent to recruits nobody.
Recruitment is outreach and follows the same rules as any other partnership
approach.

## Referral fraud arrives with the first commission payout

A referral reward is an instruction, and some affiliates follow it literally.
Three patterns recur: an affiliate referring their own second account, coupon
sites intercepting checkouts that were already going to convert, and fake
referred signups farmed for the reward.

Design the referral terms against this before launch rather than after the first
commission invoice. Pay the affiliate on activation or first payment rather than
on referred registration, since a referred registration is free to manufacture
and a payment is not. Hold commissions until the refund window passes. Cap what
one affiliate earns in a period without review. And state in the affiliate terms
which promotional methods are disallowed, so removing an affiliate is enforcement
rather than an argument.

## Measuring whether it is real

The referral that would have happened anyway is the same problem attribution has
everywhere: a partner credited for a customer who had already decided. Discount
codes make it worse, because a shopper one click from buying will search for a
code and hand the credit to whoever published one.

Count incremental customers rather than attributed ones. The blunt test is
whether total new customers rose by roughly what the program claims, or whether
the same total now arrives with commission attached to part of it. Measuring that
properly is its own discipline and is covered separately.

## When to bring in a human

Payout terms, affiliate agreements and the tax treatment of commissions are
paperwork, and paying people across several countries multiplies it. A marketing
plan should flag that rather than answer it. The same applies the first time a
partner disputes a payment, because the terms you wrote will be read closely for
the first time that day.
