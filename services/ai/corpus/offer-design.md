# Designing the offer

## The offer is not the product

The product is what you built. The offer is what someone is asked to agree to:
what they get, what it costs, how long they are committed, and what happens if
it does not work out. Two companies can ship identical products and convert at
very different rates because one of them asked for a smaller decision.

This matters because offer problems are routinely misdiagnosed as copy problems.
Rewriting a landing page cannot fix an offer that asks for too much trust too
early.

## Reduce the size of the first decision

The first commitment should be proportional to the evidence the buyer has. A
stranger who arrived from an ad has almost none, so a large first step
(long contract, high price, lengthy onboarding) fails regardless of how good the
product is.

Ways the first step gets smaller: a shorter commitment period, a narrower scope
that solves one problem rather than promising everything, or a starting point
that produces a visible result before payment is due.

For a service, the small first step is usually a booked call rather than a
proposal, because a call costs the client twenty minutes and a proposal asks them
to choose. For a membership or subscription, it is a shorter first term, since
what a stranger resists is rarely the price and is usually the length of the
commitment.

## Who carries the risk

Every offer places risk on one side. If the buyer carries all of it, they pay
before knowing whether it works, and hesitation is rational. Moving some risk
back to the seller (guarantees, refunds, cancel-anytime terms, paying only after
a result) converts better precisely because it is a costly signal: it is only
affordable to someone who expects the product to work.

The cost of honouring that risk is real and should be modelled, not assumed
away. An offer that would be ruinous if a fifth of buyers invoked it is not a
guarantee, it is a gamble.

## Time to first value decides the shape

If someone can experience the core benefit quickly and alone, let them: a
self-serve trial suits products whose value is visible in minutes.

If value only appears after configuration, data, or a change in behaviour, a
trial sets the buyer up to fail and then blames them for it. Those products
convert better through a guided first step where a human or a structured
onboarding gets them to the result.

Choosing the wrong one produces a specific symptom: high signup volume and
almost no activation.

## Price is a positioning statement

Price communicates who the product is for before any copy is read. Pricing far
below the category invites the assumption that the product is a lesser version
of something else, and attracts the buyers least likely to stay.

Founders underprice more often than they overprice, usually out of discomfort
rather than analysis. Raising price selects for buyers with the problem badly
enough to pay, who are also the buyers most likely to persist through early
roughness.

## Test the offer before optimising the funnel

Offer changes move conversion more than headline changes do, and they are
cheaper to test because they need no new assets. When conversion is
disappointing, vary the offer first, then the message, then the creative.

## A sequence for redesigning an offer

Offer work is cheap and is usually skipped because it feels less concrete than
writing copy. It has an order.

Start by writing down what is currently being asked for, in full: the price, the
commitment length, what has to happen before value arrives, what the buyer has to
supply, and what happens if it does not work. Most founders have never written
this in one place, and doing so is where the obvious problem usually appears.

Then name the largest source of hesitation. There is normally one, and it is
either price, length of commitment, effort required, or doubt that it will work
for them specifically. Each has a different remedy, which is why guessing is
expensive.

Then change one of the four and leave the rest alone. Changing several at once
makes the result uninterpretable, and offer changes are large enough that a
single variable is usually enough to move the number.

Then run it long enough to count real decisions rather than a good week.

## Choosing the shape of the first step

Four shapes cover most early-stage products, and the right one is decided by how
long value takes to appear and how much configuration it needs.

**Self-serve trial.** Correct when someone can reach the core benefit alone and
quickly. It is the cheapest to operate and the easiest to get wrong, because it
silently transfers all the onboarding work to the buyer.

**Freemium.** Correct when the product has a genuinely useful small version and
usage naturally grows into the paid one. It is wrong when the free tier is the
paid tier with an annoyance added, which teaches people the product is worse than
it is.

**Paid pilot or paid first engagement.** Correct for services and for products
requiring setup. A small paid step qualifies the buyer, funds the work, and
converts far better than a free one at the same effort, because free attracts
people with no intention of continuing.

**Money-back or outcome-based.** Correct when the value is real and the buyer's
doubt is about whether it applies to them. It moves the risk without giving the
product away.

The symptom of choosing wrongly is specific and worth watching for: high volume
of signups and almost no activation means the first step is too easy relative to
the effort the product needs. Low volume of signups and high conversion of those
who arrive means the first step is asking for too much.

## Designing a guarantee that is not a gamble

A guarantee works because it is costly to offer, so it has to be genuinely
bounded rather than dramatic.

Guarantee something you control. An outcome that depends on the buyer doing the
work is a dispute waiting to happen, and it will be invoked exactly when the
buyer did not do it. A guarantee on your delivery, your timeline, or their
ability to change their mind is enforceable and honest.

Bound it in time and scope, and say the bound plainly. A short, clear window
outperforms an unlimited promise, because an unlimited promise reads as
implausible and gets discounted.

Model the cost before offering it. Take the worst plausible rate of people
invoking it, multiply by what honouring it costs, and check that the business
survives that number. If it does not, the guarantee is not a guarantee, and the
right move is a smaller one that you can pay.

Make invoking it easy. A guarantee with an obstacle course attached costs the
same in trust as no guarantee, because the buyer assumes the obstacle course
exists.

## Pricing structure follows how value scales

The structure matters as much as the number, because it decides whether the price
grows with the value the buyer receives.

Flat pricing is simplest and is right when value does not vary much between
buyers. It is easy to communicate, which matters more early than later.

Per-seat pricing suits products where more people using it genuinely produces
more value, and it fails where it makes the buyer ration access, which is exactly
the behaviour that prevents adoption.

Usage-based pricing aligns cost with value most closely and is the hardest to
forecast on both sides. Buyers dislike unpredictable bills more than they dislike
high ones.

Tiered pricing works when the tiers are separated by something the buyer can
self-assess before purchase. Tiers separated by features nobody understands
produce paralysis, and paralysis converts at zero.

The choice is made by asking what grows when the buyer succeeds. Price on that.

## Raising the price

Founders underprice and then avoid correcting it, usually because they imagine
raising it as a single event affecting everyone.

It is not. New buyers pay the new price; existing customers can be left where
they are indefinitely, and often should be, because the goodwill is worth more
than the difference. If existing prices do move, give real notice, honour the old
price for a period, and say what is changing rather than dressing it as an
improvement.

The evidence that a price is too low is rarely a complaint. It is the absence of
one. If essentially nobody hesitates at the price, the price is not doing any
selecting, and the buyers it is failing to filter out are the ones who cost the
most to serve.

Test upward on new traffic before deciding. A price increase that holds
conversion is free margin, and a price increase that halves conversion has told
you something about the positioning rather than about the price.

## One offer at a time

Presenting several offers to a stranger asks them to make two decisions, whether
to buy and which to buy, and the second one is where people stop. Lead with one,
and let the alternatives be discoverable for the minority who want them.

The same applies to bundling. A bundle that solves one problem more completely is
an improvement. A bundle assembled to justify a price is discounted by the buyer
to the value of the one component they wanted.

## Testing an offer when there is no traffic

Offer changes can be evaluated well before there is enough volume for a split
test, which is fortunate, because there rarely is.

Ask the people who did not buy. The reason for hesitation is a stronger signal
than any conversion rate at low volume, and it is usually given plainly when the
question is asked without defending the product.

Offer the alternative directly in conversation. A dozen sales conversations
offering a shorter commitment tell you more, faster, than a page test that will
not reach significance for a quarter.

Watch what people ask for. Repeated requests to pay differently, commit for less
time, or start smaller are the offer telling you its shape, before any test does.
