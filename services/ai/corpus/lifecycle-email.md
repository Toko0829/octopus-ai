# Lifecycle email for a small paid product

## Email earns its place by being owned

Email is the only channel in the funnel that is not rented. Algorithm changes and
ad auction shifts do not take the list away, which is why building it early
matters more than optimising it early.

## The sequences that carry most of the weight

**Welcome.** Sent immediately on signup, when intent is highest. Its job is to
deliver whatever was promised at capture, set expectations for frequency, and
get the reader to a first meaningful action in the product.

The trigger is whatever the person just did, and it is worth naming precisely,
because the promise to honour differs. A registration on a waitlist earns news
about the launch. A booked call earns the details of the call. An enquiry earns
an answer. Sending the same welcome to all of them wastes the one moment when
attention is guaranteed.

**Activation.** For trials and freemium, the goal is one specific action that
correlates with sticking around. Identify the behaviour that separates retained
users from churned ones, then build the sequence around producing that behaviour
rather than around describing features.

**Conversion.** For trials, a short sequence before expiry that addresses the
objection rather than repeating the offer. Discounting is the last lever, not the
first, because it trains the audience to wait for discounts.

**Winback.** A bounded sequence for lapsed users, with a clear stop. Repeatedly
mailing people who have disengaged damages deliverability and costs more in
reputation than the recovered revenue is worth.

For a membership or community product the same sequence runs against members who
have stopped showing up, and the honest version asks what changed rather than
announcing a discount.

## Deliverability is a prerequisite, not an optimisation

Authenticate the sending domain, warm it gradually rather than sending a large
first campaign, and remove hard bounces immediately. Sending to people who never
asked to hear from you is the fastest way to a permanent reputation problem.

Prune unengaged subscribers on a schedule. A smaller list that opens is worth
more than a large one that does not, both commercially and for inbox placement.

## Consent differs by market

Consent requirements are stricter in the EU and UK than in the US, and the
rules attach to the recipient's location rather than the sender's. A list that
mixes markets should be handled to the strictest applicable standard unless it
is segmented by jurisdiction.

## Measure revenue, not opens

Open rates became unreliable once mail clients began pre-fetching images. Click
behaviour and downstream revenue per recipient are the metrics worth optimising.

## Building the list before optimising the sending

A sequence sent to nobody is a sequence that has not been tested, and most early
programmes are held back by capture rather than by writing.

Capture requires a reason, and the reason has to be specific. "Subscribe for
updates" asks the reader to pay attention for an unspecified future benefit,
which almost nobody agrees to. Something concrete and immediately useful, aimed
at the same problem the product solves, converts many times better and selects
for people who have that problem.

Put the ask where the person has just been helped rather than where they arrived.
The end of a piece that answered their question is the moment they are most
willing; a banner on arrival is the moment they are least.

Ask for as little as the next step needs, which for a list is usually an email
address alone. Every additional field trades registrations for information you
have no use for until much later.

Never buy or scrape a list. The people on it did not ask to hear from you, the
complaints that follow are what create a permanent reputation problem, and the
damage is not confined to that campaign.

## Writing the welcome

The welcome is the one message that will reliably be opened, and most are wasted
on introducing the company.

Deliver what was promised in the first message, immediately and without a
condition. If the promise was a guide, the guide is in that message rather than
behind a further step.

Say who is writing and how often they will write. Expectations set here are what
prevent unsubscribes later, and a stated frequency is easier to honour than an
implied one.

Ask for one small thing, chosen for what the person just did. Somebody who joined
a waiting list can be asked what they are hoping it will solve, and the replies
are the best positioning research available anywhere.

Send it from a person, and let replies actually go to that person. An address
that discards replies wastes the only channel in the funnel where the audience
can talk back.

Write it as one message that stands alone. A welcome split across five parts
assumes a level of interest that has not been earned yet.

## Activation: finding the action worth driving

The activation sequence is the highest-value one and it is the one that cannot be
written without a specific fact: which single behaviour separates the people who
stay from the people who leave.

Find it by comparing the two groups rather than by reasoning about the product.
Look at what retained users did in their first days that churned users did not.
It is usually smaller and more specific than expected, and it is often not the
feature the team considers central.

Then build the sequence around producing that behaviour. Each message drives to
that one action, from a different angle, rather than touring the features.

Stop the sequence when the person does it. Continuing to prompt somebody who has
already acted is how a helpful programme becomes an irritating one, and it is a
common defect because it requires the sending to know what the product knows.

Where the sequence consistently fails to produce the action, the problem is
usually in the product's first-run experience rather than in the email, and more
messages will not fix it.

## Conversion, and where discounting belongs

For a trial or a free tier, the conversion sequence runs before the moment of
decision rather than after it.

Address the objection rather than repeating the offer. The person has already
seen the offer, and the reason they have not acted is a specific doubt: whether
it works for their case, whether they will have time, whether they can get their
data out again. Each is answerable in a message.

Remind them what they will lose, concretely. Work they have already done in the
product is more persuasive than any feature list, and it is specific to them.

Make the mechanics obvious. A surprising number of non-conversions are people who
did not realise the trial was ending or could not find how to continue.

Discount last, if at all. A discount offered routinely at the end of every trial
becomes the price, and it teaches the audience to wait. An offer of more time is
usually a better lever and costs nothing.

## Winback, bounded

A lapsed-user sequence is short, has a stated end, and asks rather than
announces.

Ask what changed. It gets replies, and the replies are the clearest information
about why the product loses people, which no dashboard reports.

Say plainly that this is the last message unless they want to hear more, and mean
it. A bounded sequence recovers roughly what an unbounded one does and does not
damage the sending reputation.

Remove the people who do not respond. Continuing to mail people who have
disengaged is the largest controllable threat to inbox placement, and it costs
more in deliverability for the whole list than the recovered revenue is worth.

## Deliverability, in the order it matters

Authenticate the sending domain before the first campaign. This is the
prerequisite that determines whether anything else has an effect.

Use a subdomain for marketing sending, kept separate from the domain that sends
transactional and personal mail, so a marketing reputation problem cannot take
the receipts and password resets with it.

Warm a new sending domain gradually. A large first campaign from a domain with no
history is the pattern that filters are built to catch.

Remove hard bounces immediately and unsubscribes instantly. Both are treated as
signals of a sender who is not paying attention.

Prune the unengaged on a schedule. A smaller list that opens is worth more than a
large one that does not, commercially and for placement, and the pruning is what
keeps the engaged half arriving in the inbox.

Make the unsubscribe easy and obvious. A hidden unsubscribe converts into a spam
complaint, which is far more damaging than the departure.

## Consent, and why the strictest standard usually wins

Consent requirements are stricter in the EU and UK than in the US, and they
attach to the recipient's location rather than the sender's. A list that mixes
markets should be handled to the strictest applicable standard unless it is
genuinely segmented by jurisdiction, because the alternative is a segmentation
that has to be right every time.

The practical implications are the same ones that improve results anyway: consent
that is given rather than assumed, a record of when and how it was given, a
purpose the person would recognise, and a way out that works. Where a campaign
gates real sending into a regulated market, that is a point to verify against
current guidance rather than to infer from a marketing document.

## Measuring

Open rates became unreliable once mail clients began pre-fetching images, and a
programme optimised for opens is optimised for a number that no longer measures
attention.

Measure clicks, the next step taken after the click, and revenue per recipient.
The last is the one that decides whether a sequence is worth keeping.

Measure per sequence rather than in aggregate. A welcome and a winback have
completely different jobs and averaging them hides both.

Watch unsubscribes and complaints as a cost, alongside the revenue. A campaign
that produces revenue and burns the list is borrowing from next quarter.
