# Positioning and choosing who you are for

## Positioning is upstream of every channel

Positioning decides who the product is for and why they should care. Every
downstream activity inherits it: ads, content, landing pages, email. When
positioning is wrong, no channel tactic rescues it, and the failure is usually
misread as a channel problem. Teams respond by changing creative, then
audiences, then platforms, and the numbers stay flat because the message was
never aimed at anyone in particular.

## The diagnostic

Two patterns point at positioning rather than execution:

Ads earn clicks but not conversions. The hook is interesting enough to
interrupt someone, and the promise underneath does not match what they wanted.

Content earns traffic but not signups. The topic attracts readers who were never
going to buy, which usually means the content was chosen for reach rather than
for the problem the product solves.

The service version of the same symptom is a steady trickle of enquiries from
people who cannot afford the work, or clients who arrive expecting something
adjacent to what is sold. That is not a sales problem to be handled better at the
call, it is the positioning selecting the wrong people before the call.

In both cases more budget makes the loss larger, not smaller.

## Narrow converts better than broad

Broad positioning feels safer because it appears to keep every opportunity open.
In practice it competes with everyone and speaks specifically to no one. A
narrower statement of who the product is for reduces the addressable audience on
paper and raises conversion enough to more than compensate at early scale.

Narrowing is a claim about the problem, not a demographic filter. "For people
who already tried the manual approach and hit a wall" is a sharper boundary than
an age range or a job title, because it selects on a situation the reader can
recognise in themselves.

## Where the answer comes from

Positioning is discovered, not invented. The sources worth mining, in order:

People who already bought. What did they try before, and what pushed them to
switch on the day they switched?

People who churned. What did they expect that did not arrive?

People who considered and did not buy. This group is the hardest to reach and
the most informative, because they saw the pitch and disagreed with it.

Competitor reviews describing frustration are the cheapest substitute when no
customer base exists yet.

## Write it down as a testable sentence

State who it is for, the situation they are in, what changes for them, and what
they would otherwise do. Anything that would still read as true if a competitor
published it is not positioning, it is category description.

Then treat it as a hypothesis. Positioning is validated by whether strangers
convert, not by whether it sounds right internally.

## When to bring in a human

Positioning work benefits from someone who is not attached to the product.
Founders are too close to their own build to hear which parts are ordinary. A
strategist earns their fee here more reliably than anywhere else in the funnel,
because the decision affects every downstream cost.

## Doing the interviews

The sources above are only useful if the conversations produce specifics, and
most do not, because the questions invite opinion. Opinion about a product is
nearly worthless. What happened is not.

Ask about the last time rather than about preferences in general. "What were you
using before, and what happened on the day you decided to change" produces a
story with a trigger in it. "What do you look for in a tool like this" produces a
list of adjectives that every competitor also satisfies.

Ask what they tried first, because the thing someone tried before finding you is
the real competitor, and it is usually a manual workaround rather than a rival
product. A positioning statement aimed at a rival product while buyers are
actually leaving a spreadsheet is aimed at the wrong place.

Ask what nearly stopped them. The hesitation is the objection your landing page
has to answer, and people will describe it plainly in conversation while never
writing it in a form.

Ask how they described it to somebody else afterwards. Their sentence is usually
better than yours, and it is written in the vocabulary the market actually uses.

A handful of conversations is enough to see a pattern. Beyond about a dozen the
same three answers repeat, and the marginal one is not worth the calendar time.
Record the person's own words rather than a summary, because the words are the
deliverable.

## Turning it into a sentence

The statement has four parts, and the discipline is that each is falsifiable.

Who it is for, stated as a situation rather than a demographic. The situation
should be one the reader could confirm or deny about themselves in a second.

What they are doing today, named specifically. This is what you are actually
competing with.

What changes, stated as an outcome rather than a feature. A feature is what the
product does; an outcome is what becomes true for the person.

Why now, which is the trigger that turns a background annoyance into a purchase.
A statement with no "why now" describes a vitamin, and vitamins are bought
slowly.

Worked through, for a scheduling tool: "For a solo consultant who is still
arranging calls over email, this replaces the back-and-forth with a link, so the
booking happens in one message instead of five. The trigger is the first week
where the arranging took longer than the calls."

Test the result by handing it to someone outside the company and asking who it is
not for. If they cannot say, it is not positioning yet.

## Choosing between several plausible audiences

Most products can serve more than one kind of buyer, and the instinct is to keep
all of them. That is what produces a message aimed at nobody.

Choose one to lead with, on four criteria. Which group feels the problem most
urgently, since urgency is what makes a stranger act. Which group is reachable,
because an audience you cannot find cheaply is not available at this stage
whatever their fit. Which group can pay what the product needs to charge. And
which group, if delighted, talks to others like them, because a segment with
dense internal referral compounds and a scattered one does not.

Leading with one is not abandoning the others. It decides what the homepage says
and what the ads claim, both of which have to say something specific to work at
all. The others remain welcome and simply are not the argument.

## Repositioning without losing the customers you have

Repositioning is usually necessary before it is comfortable, and the fear is that
current customers will feel abandoned. In practice they mostly do not notice,
because they are already using the product and are not reading the homepage.

The sequence that works: change the acquisition surfaces first, since those are
aimed at strangers. Leave the product's existing language alone until the new
positioning has evidence behind it. Tell existing customers only if something
they rely on is actually changing, and if nothing is, say nothing, because
announcing a repositioning invites people to worry about a change that is not
happening to them.

Where a segment is genuinely being dropped, say so plainly and early. The cost of
a small number of people leaving on good terms is much lower than the cost of
them discovering it when a feature disappears.

## Failure shapes worth recognising

**Positioning as feature list.** The page describes what the product does and
never says who should care. It reads as complete and converts as though it said
nothing, because the reader has to do the work of deciding whether it is for
them.

**Positioning against a competitor.** Defining the product as the better version
of a known thing borrows that thing's category and its buying criteria, which are
set by the incumbent to favour the incumbent. It also fails entirely with buyers
who have never heard of the competitor, which is most of them.

**Positioning on a demographic.** Age, job title and company size are convenient
because ad platforms accept them as inputs, and they are not what makes someone
buy. Two people with the same job title, one of whom has hit the wall you solve
and one of whom has not, are not the same prospect.

**Positioning on being cheaper.** It is the easiest claim to make and the easiest
to beat, it attracts the buyers least likely to stay, and it is the one claim
that says nothing about the problem.

**Positioning that everyone in the category could publish.** The single most
useful edit is to read the statement and ask whether a competitor could put their
name on it unchanged. If they could, it is category description.

## How you know it is working

Positioning is validated by strangers, not by internal agreement, and the
evidence arrives in a particular order.

First, in conversation: people finish your sentence, or say some version of "that
is exactly what happens to me". The recognition is the signal.

Then, in the funnel: the same traffic converts at a higher rate, or the same
spend produces enquiries from better-fitting people. Note that the volume of
enquiries can fall while the positioning improves, because part of what it is
doing is turning away the wrong ones earlier.

Then, in sales conversations: the objection changes. Weak positioning produces
"what is this", and good positioning produces "how much" and "how soon". A change
in the kind of question being asked is a better signal than any single conversion
rate, and it arrives sooner.
