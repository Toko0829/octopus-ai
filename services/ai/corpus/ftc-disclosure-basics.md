# Disclosure obligations when promoting a product (US)

> Informational only, not legal advice. Advertising rules change and depend on
> the specifics of a campaign. Anything that gates a real launch should be
> verified against the current regulator guidance and, where the stakes justify
> it, reviewed by a qualified professional.

## The underlying principle

US advertising regulation centres on a simple idea: advertising must not be
deceptive, and a material connection between an endorser and the thing they are
endorsing must be disclosed. "Material connection" covers payment, free product,
discounts, employment, and family relationships. The test is whether a reasonable
consumer would want to know about the relationship when weighing the endorsement.

## What makes a disclosure adequate

A disclosure has to be noticed and understood by an ordinary viewer:

- Placed where the endorsement is, not on a separate page, in a profile bio, or
  behind a "more" link.
- In the same medium as the claim. A spoken endorsement needs a spoken or
  clearly superimposed disclosure, not only text in a description.
- In plain language. Ambiguous tags are weaker than saying plainly that the post
  is an advertisement or that the product was received free.
- Present regardless of platform tooling. A platform's built-in paid-partnership
  label may not be sufficient on its own.

## Claims about results

Testimonials describing outcomes invite the question of what results a typical
person should expect. Claims that cannot be substantiated are the highest-risk
part of a campaign, and endorsements implying atypical results generally require
either substantiation or clear context about typical outcomes.

## AI-generated content

Content generated or heavily assisted by AI is still advertising and carries the
same truthfulness obligations. Some platforms separately require labelling of
synthetic media, and those platform rules apply on top of advertising law rather
than instead of it.

## Sector-specific rules exist

Health, financial, alcohol, and children's advertising carry additional
requirements that are stricter than the general rules. Treat any campaign in
those categories as requiring review before launch rather than after.

## Who carries the obligation

Both sides do, and the assumption that it belongs to the creator alone is the
most common way a small advertiser gets it wrong.

The advertiser is responsible for what is said on its behalf. That means the
brief has to state the disclosure requirement and the claims that may not be
made, and somebody has to actually look at the published material afterwards. A
brief that omits it has delegated a legal obligation to somebody who may never
have been told it exists.

The endorser is responsible for disclosing their own relationship. That includes
relationships that are not cash: free product, a discount, early access,
affiliate commission, employment, and family connections all count.

Employees posting about their own employer are endorsers too, which surprises
people. A team member recommending the product without saying where they work is
the same undisclosed material connection as a paid creator.

## Where and how a disclosure appears

The working test is whether an ordinary viewer would notice and understand it in
the normal course of consuming the content, not whether it exists somewhere.

**In the content, not around it.** A profile bio, a separate page, a comment
below, or text that only appears after expanding a caption are all places the
viewer may never reach.

**Before the endorsement, not after it.** A disclosure at the end of a video has
missed most of the audience, and it has certainly missed the moment the
recommendation was received.

**In the same medium as the claim.** A spoken endorsement needs a spoken or
clearly superimposed disclosure. Text in a description does not carry a video
that is watched with the description collapsed.

**Legible.** Large enough, on screen long enough to read, and in a colour that
contrasts with what is behind it. A disclosure that is technically present and
practically invisible is the shape most likely to be treated as inadequate.

**Repeated in a long piece**, especially where the audience may join partway
through, as with live content.

**In plain language.** Saying plainly that a post is an advertisement, or that a
product was received free, is stronger than an abbreviation or a tag whose
meaning the audience has to know.

**Independently of platform tooling.** A built-in paid-partnership label may not
be sufficient on its own, and it is not visible everywhere the content travels.

## The failure shapes that recur

**Disclosure buried in a hashtag block.** Placed at the end among a dozen other
tags, where it is designed not to be read.

**Ambiguous wording.** Terms that could mean several things, or that assume the
reader knows a convention, are weaker than the plain statement.

**Relying on the audience already knowing.** A creator who works with a brand
frequently still discloses each time, because each post reaches people who have
not seen the others.

**Disclosing the payment and not the free product.** Gifted product with no fee
is a material connection, and it is the case most often treated as though it were
not.

**Assuming an affiliate link is self-evident.** Commission on a link is a
material connection and is disclosed like any other.

**Discovering the requirement after publication.** Editing a disclosure into a
post afterwards does not reach the people who already saw it, and on some
surfaces it is not possible at all.

## Claims about results

Testimonials describing outcomes invite the question of what results a typical
person should expect. Claims that cannot be substantiated are the highest-risk
part of a campaign, and endorsements implying atypical results generally require
either substantiation or clear context about typical outcomes.

Substantiation means evidence held before the claim is published, not evidence
that could be assembled if somebody asked. The practical version for a small
advertiser is to keep, alongside each claim used in marketing, the specific thing
it rests on: the measurement, the dated case, the customer who agreed to it.

A disclaimer does not rescue a claim the material contradicts. Where the main
message says one thing and small print says another, the main message is what the
audience took away.

Comparisons to competitors and superlatives are both claims about fact and both
need the same backing.

## Reviews and testimonials you did not commission

Incentivised reviews carry the same disclosure obligation as any other
endorsement, and asking only satisfied customers for reviews while discouraging
others is its own problem.

Reposting a customer's positive review into your own advertising makes it your
claim. The obligations attach to it once you have published it, including whether
the outcome it describes is typical.

Suppressing negative reviews on a surface you control, or writing reviews of your
own product, are the two behaviours least likely to survive scrutiny and most
likely to be discovered.

## AI-generated content

Content generated or heavily assisted by AI is still advertising and carries the
same truthfulness obligations. Some platforms separately require labelling of
synthetic media, and those platform rules apply on top of advertising law rather
than instead of it.

Two specific risks come with generated material. It produces confident specifics,
including numbers and comparisons, that nobody verified and that would count as
unsubstantiated claims. And synthetic depictions of a real person, including a
voice, raise separate questions about that person's rights that have nothing to
do with advertising law.

The practical control is that generated marketing copy is reviewed for factual
claims before publication by somebody who knows what the business can actually
substantiate.

## Sector-specific rules exist

Health, financial, alcohol, and children's advertising carry additional
requirements that are stricter than the general rules. Treat any campaign in
those categories as requiring review before launch rather than after.

## What to do before a campaign goes live

A short checklist, applied to each piece rather than to the campaign as a whole.

Is every factual claim in it something the business can substantiate today, and
is the evidence written down somewhere findable?

Does every endorsement carry a disclosure, placed where the endorsement is, in
the same medium, before it, and in plain words?

Has the brief given to every partner stated both the disclosure requirement and
the claims that must not be made?

Has somebody looked at what was actually published, rather than at what was
briefed?

Does the campaign touch a regulated category, in which case it goes for review
before launch rather than after?

Anything that gates a real launch, and anything in a regulated category, should
be verified against current regulator guidance rather than against this document.
