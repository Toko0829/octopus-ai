# SEO when nobody is searching for your product yet

## The category-search trap

A new product usually has no search demand for its own name, and the category
terms are held by established competitors with years of accumulated authority.
Competing head-on for those terms is the most common way early SEO effort is
wasted: the content is fine, and it never ranks.

## Target the problem, not the category

Search demand exists for the problem the product solves, expressed in the words
people use before they know a solution category exists. Those queries are longer,
lower in volume, and far less contested. They also convert better, because
someone describing their specific situation is closer to acting than someone
typing a broad category term.

This is why organic search tends to produce fewer visitors and better ones. A
page that answers a real question earns enquiries and signups from people who
arrived already believing they have the problem, which is the expensive half of
persuasion done before they landed.

## Match the intent behind the query

A query asking how to do something wants instructions, and a page that is mostly
a pitch will lose to one that actually answers. Earn the right to mention the
product by being genuinely the best answer to the question first.

## Depth beats frequency

A small number of thorough pages that fully answer a question outperform a large
number of shallow ones. Shallow content published on a schedule accumulates
maintenance cost without accumulating authority.

## SEO is slow, and that is a planning constraint

Results compound over months, not weeks. That makes SEO a poor choice as the
only channel for an early launch and a good complement to paid, which buys
immediate signal while organic accrues. Plan them together: paid reveals which
messages convert, and those messages inform what content is worth writing.

## Technical basics that actually matter early

Crawlable pages, sensible titles, one clear topic per page, and pages that load
quickly on a phone. Beyond that, most technical SEO effort at this stage is
displacement activity compared with writing something worth ranking.

## What AI-generated content does not change

Search engines reward content that helps the reader regardless of how it was
produced. Volume without substance was already a losing strategy, and generating
it faster does not change the outcome.

## Finding the queries when the tools say zero

Keyword tools report volume for terms they have enough data about, and the
queries worth targeting early are usually below that floor. Reported volume of
zero is not evidence of no demand, it is evidence of no data, and treating it as
the former is what sends founders back to the contested category terms.

The sources that work instead are all records of somebody actually asking.

The questions arriving in sales conversations, support requests and the message
people send before buying. These are queries by definition, and they are phrased
the way the person would type them.

The completions and related searches a search engine offers while typing the
problem. Those are generated from real queries, including ones too small to
appear in a volume report.

Question threads in communities where your buyers gather. A question with several
replies is a page nobody has written well.

Competitor reviews describing what people were trying to do. The frustration is
usually stated as a task, and the task is the query.

The site's own internal search, once there is one, and the terms already bringing
any traffic at all, which are usually not the terms the pages were written for.

Take the phrasing verbatim rather than tidying it. The tidy version is the
contested one.

## Choosing which query to write for

Judge a query on three things and skip the volume estimate.

Whether the person typing it has the problem the product solves. A large query
adjacent to the problem brings readers who will never convert and is worse than
no traffic, because it costs the effort of writing.

Whether you can be the best answer. Look at what currently ranks: if the existing
answers are thorough and written by people with more authority, that page is not
winnable this year. If they are thin, generic, or clearly written by somebody who
has not done the thing, it is.

Whether the answer leads naturally to the product. Not every useful page needs
to, but a plan made entirely of pages with no path to a next step produces
traffic and nothing else.

Write the page for one query and its close variants. A page trying to rank for
several distinct questions answers none of them first, which is what the
searcher and the ranking both reward.

## Page types, and what each is for

**The direct answer.** One question, answered immediately and thoroughly. The
backbone of early organic, and the type most likely to rank because most existing
answers are padded.

**The how-to, done manually.** Instructions for doing the thing the product
automates. It converts unusually well, because the reader experiences the problem
while following it.

**The comparison.** People compare before buying, and if you do not write it
somebody else writes it about you. It only works if it is honest about where the
alternative is better, because a comparison that finds you superior on every axis
is read as an advertisement and dismissed.

**The alternatives page.** People searching for alternatives to a known product
are in-market and describing their situation precisely. Worth writing once, kept
accurate.

**The glossary or explainer.** Low intent, useful for building topical coverage,
and easy to over-invest in. A handful is fine; a hundred is displacement
activity.

What does not belong in an early plan: pages targeting the category head term,
and pages written because a competitor has one.

## Structure that helps and structure that does not

The part of technical SEO that matters early is short. Every page should be
reachable by following links from the home page, have one clear topic, carry a
title that states that topic in the words people search for, and load quickly on
a phone.

Link related pages to each other in the body, with descriptive wording rather
than "read more". This is the cheapest structural improvement available and it
also helps readers.

Do not build a large empty structure in advance. Categories and hubs are worth
creating when there is enough content to fill them, and before that they are
pages with nothing on them.

Everything beyond this, at this stage, is displacement activity compared with
writing something worth ranking.

## Maintaining what is published

Search content is an asset that decays, and the maintenance is a small fraction
of the cost of writing.

Update rather than republish. A page that has accumulated any standing keeps it
when it is improved and starts over when it is replaced at a new address.

Review the pages that rank on the second page of results before writing anything
new. A page close to the top is usually cheaper to move than a new page is to
create, and the improvement is normally that it answers the question less
directly than the ones above it.

Fix pages that get traffic and produce nothing. Traffic arriving and leaving is
a next step missing, not a ranking problem.

Delete or merge pages that were written thin. They cost credibility and they
compete with your own better pages.

## When SEO is the wrong investment

Organic search is slow, and slow is fine when there is time and wrong when there
is not.

It is the wrong first channel when the product needs signal this quarter,
because months of writing produces nothing decision-useful in that window.

It is the wrong channel when the problem is genuinely not searched for, which
happens with products solving something people have not named. Those products are
found through distribution and partnerships, and forcing search content produces
pages about an adjacent problem that attract the wrong readers.

It is the wrong channel when positioning is unsettled, because the pages encode
the positioning and will need rewriting.

It is worth starting early, at low volume, alongside a channel that produces
signal faster. Paid reveals which messages convert, and those messages are the
brief for what is worth writing.
