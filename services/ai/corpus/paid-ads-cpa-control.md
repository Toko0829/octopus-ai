# Controlling cost per acquisition on paid social

## Start with a testing budget, not a scaling budget

An early campaign's job is to buy information, not customers. Set a fixed test
budget you are willing to lose entirely and treat it as research spend. Scaling
decisions come after you have a signal, not before.

Decide first what event the cost is being counted against, and write that down
too. For one product the acquisition is a purchase, for another it is a signup, a
qualified lead, a booked call or an app install. A CPA quoted without naming its
event is not comparable to anything, including your own figure from last month,
and the cheapest-looking channel is usually the one counting the easiest event.

Set a CPA ceiling before launch, derived from unit economics rather than from
what feels affordable. If a customer is worth 40 units of margin over the period
you are willing to wait for payback, a CPA above that is a loss you are choosing
to repeat. Write the ceiling down; it becomes the guardrail that pauses the
campaign automatically.

Where the counted event sits earlier in the funnel than the payment, the ceiling
has to carry the conversion rate between them. Paying 5 units for a lead that
becomes a customer one time in ten is paying 50 units for the customer.

## Test hooks, not audiences, first

On platforms with strong automated targeting, creative is the lever that moves
performance most, and audience configuration matters less than it used to. Run
several distinct hooks against the same audience rather than the same creative
against several audiences. Distinct means a different claim or angle, not a
different colour of button.

Give each variant enough budget to exit the learning phase. Splitting a small
budget across many variants produces many inconclusive results rather than one
clear answer.

## Kill losers early, scale winners slowly

Underperforming variants should be paused on a schedule you set in advance, so
the decision is not made emotionally while watching a dashboard. Increase budget
on winners in modest steps; large jumps reset the platform's optimisation and
typically degrade the performance you were trying to buy more of.

## Attribution will disagree with itself

Platform-reported conversions and your own analytics will not match, because
they attribute differently and platforms count conversions they influenced.
Pick one source as the number of record for decisions, usually your own, and
use platform numbers only for relative comparison between variants inside the
same platform.

## When to bring in a human

Sustained underperformance after several creative iterations is usually a
positioning problem rather than an ads problem, and no amount of budget
reallocation fixes it. That is the point to route the work to a strategist
rather than continuing to iterate creative.

## A launch sequence, and the calendar that goes with it

The order below exists so that each decision is made once, with the information
that decision needs, rather than continuously while watching a dashboard.

Before anything is spent, write down four numbers: the event you are counting,
what one of those events is worth in margin, the CPA ceiling that follows from
it, and the total test budget you are willing to lose. Those four are the whole
guardrail, and writing them down is what makes the later decisions arithmetic
rather than nerve.

Launch with several distinct concepts against one audience, each with enough
budget to accumulate a meaningful number of the counted event. Splitting a small
budget across many variants is the most common way a test ends with nothing
learned: every variant gets a handful of results, and a handful cannot separate
a good hook from a lucky one.

Then leave it alone for a fixed period, decided in advance. Early numbers move
violently and mean almost nothing, and the platform's own optimisation is still
settling. Every intervention during that window resets what was being learned and
costs the budget already spent on it.

At the end of the window, make one decision per concept: keep, kill, or iterate.
Concepts far above the ceiling are killed. Concepts near it are iterated, since
a near miss usually means the idea works and the execution is heavy. Concepts
under it get more budget, in modest increments.

Then repeat with new concepts. The library is inventory, and a campaign that
stops being fed new ideas decays.

## When the cost per acquisition is too high

This is the most common question in paid acquisition and it has an order. Work
down it rather than reaching for the budget slider, because the first two causes
are far more likely than the last and far cheaper to fix.

**Check the event first.** A CPA is a ratio, and a broken denominator produces a
frightening number from a healthy campaign. Confirm that the conversion is firing
once per real occurrence, that it is not counting a refresh or a returning
visitor twice, and that the platform and your own analytics are at least counting
the same event. A tracking fault presents as a sudden CPA change with no change
in what people are doing.

**Then check the ceiling.** A CPA that is too high against a ceiling derived from
what felt affordable is not a campaign problem. Recompute it from margin, and
carry the conversion rate between the counted event and the payment if they are
different: a signup that becomes a customer one time in five and a customer worth
fifty units of margin gives a signup ceiling of ten, not fifty.

**Then look at where the cost is concentrated.** Cost per acquisition is the
product of what it costs to buy a click and the rate at which clicks convert.
Those two fail differently and are fixed in different places. Expensive clicks
with healthy conversion is a creative and auction problem: the hook is not
earning attention and you are paying for reach you have not earned. Cheap clicks
with poor conversion is a message-match or offer problem, and the fix is on the
landing page or in the offer, not in the ad account.

**Then look at the offer.** If a campaign is asking a stranger for a large first
commitment, no amount of creative iteration makes that decision smaller. This is
the point where a paid problem turns out to be an offer problem.

**Then consider that it may be positioning.** Sustained underperformance across
several genuinely different concepts, each of which was competently made, is the
signature. More budget and more assets do not resolve it.

Only after all of that is throttling the budget the right response, and even then
it treats the symptom.

## The arithmetic, worked

Suppose a customer pays 60 units and the cost of serving them for the period you
are willing to wait is 20, leaving 40 units of margin. That 40 is the absolute
ceiling on customer acquisition cost, and spending exactly it buys growth at zero
profit, which is a choice rather than an accident.

If the campaign counts signups rather than customers, and one signup in eight
becomes a customer, the signup ceiling is 5 units. A campaign reporting a signup
cost of 4 is inside the ceiling. One reporting 12 is losing money on every signup
whatever the volume looks like.

Now suppose the test budget is 400 units. At a 5-unit target that is roughly 80
signups if everything works, which is enough to distinguish a strong concept from
a weak one and not enough to distinguish two similar executions. That number is
what should decide how many variants launch, rather than how many assets happen
to exist.

If the same 400 is split across ten variants, each gets 40 units and about eight
signups. Eight is noise. This is the arithmetic behind testing fewer, more
different things.

## Structure: fewer campaigns than feels natural

Consolidated campaigns generally outperform fragmented ones on platforms with
automated optimisation, because every split divides the data the optimiser
learns from. A campaign per audience segment, per placement and per creative
format produces many campaigns that each know very little.

Split only where the split reflects a decision you would actually make
differently: a genuinely different market, a genuinely different offer, or a
budget you need to ring-fence. Splitting to inspect performance is not a reason,
because the reporting can be read without dividing the spend.

## Scaling without resetting what you bought

Increasing budget changes the problem the optimiser is solving, and a large jump
makes it start over. Raise in modest steps, allow the campaign to settle between
them, and accept that performance usually degrades somewhat as spend rises,
because the cheapest available audience is bought first.

That degradation is expected rather than a fault. Plan for the CPA at the budget
you intend to reach, not the CPA at the budget where the test ran. A concept that
works only at small spend is a useful finding and not a growth channel.

The alternative to raising budget on one winner is running a second concept
alongside it. That usually scales better, because it buys new attention rather
than more frequency against the same people.

## Fatigue, and how to tell it from a broken campaign

Performance decaying gradually on a stable audience and a stable offer is usually
fatigue: the same people have seen the asset too many times. The tell is that the
cost of buying attention rises while the conversion rate of the people who do
arrive stays roughly flat.

A campaign that breaks rather than fatigues looks different. The change is
abrupt, and it usually coincides with something that was edited, a policy
decision, an approval, or a tracking change.

Fatigue is answered with new concepts, not with budget changes and not with a
new colour of the same asset. A campaign that breaks is answered by finding what
changed.

## What to do with the results after the test

The purpose of a test budget is information, so the information has to be
recorded somewhere other than the ad account, which reorganises itself and
eventually deletes things. For each concept, keep the claim it made, what it
cost per counted event, and one sentence about why you think it did that.

The value compounds. After a handful of tests the pattern in which claims work is
worth more than any individual winner, and it feeds positioning, landing pages
and email rather than only the next campaign.
