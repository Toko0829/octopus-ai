# Measuring an early-stage product

## Measure decisions, not activity

A number earns its place by changing something you would otherwise do. Before
adding one, answer the question it exists to settle: if this halved next week,
what would you do differently? A number with no answer to that is a cost, because
somebody maintains it and everybody glances at it.

This is why most early dashboards are worse than a short list written by hand
each week. They report activity, which is always available, instead of the two or
three quantities that would change the plan.

## One north-star, a few inputs

Pick one number that means the business is working, and a few inputs that feed
it. The north-star is money, or the commitment that reliably precedes money.

The distinction that matters is attention versus commitment. Impressions, views,
follower counts and raw traffic measure attention, which is borrowed and predicts
little on its own. Signups, registrations, enquiries, booked calls, installs,
trials, subscribers and paying customers are commitments, because each one cost
the person something, even if only an email address and a moment of trust.

Give every input one owner. A number belonging to every channel belongs to none
of them, and when it moves nobody can say why.

## The numbers will disagree, and that is normal

Every advertising platform reports conversions it believes it influenced, your
own analytics counts differently, and the bank account knows something else
again. The gap is often large enough to change what you would spend.

It is rarely a bug. The platforms answer a different question: who saw their
advertising and later converted, which includes people who would have converted
anyway. Pick the most conservative source as the number of record, and use each
platform's own figures only to compare variants inside that same platform, where
the counting is at least consistent. Choose which source is authoritative before
the disagreement appears, or the most flattering number wins the argument by
default. Acting on a cost per acquisition once you have chosen is a separate
discipline with its own guardrails.

## Track few events, and name them once

Instrument the commitment moments and little else: an account created, a
registration completed, a call booked, a purchase made. Each gets one name,
decided before launch and written down. Renaming later severs the history either
side of the rename, which is how a founder ends up unable to compare quarters.

Two properties are worth insisting on. An event should fire once per real
occurrence, so a refresh does not inflate it, and it should carry enough context
to say which campaign, page or partner produced it. A total with no breakdown
cannot direct any spending.

## Incrementality: what would have happened anyway

No attribution model answers whether the customer would have arrived without the
spending. Some of the people a platform claims credit for were going to buy
regardless, and a report built from clicks cannot separate them.

The cheap approximations are good enough to act on. Pause a channel for a week
and watch the total rather than that channel's own figure. Hold back a region or
a time window and compare. Ask new customers how they came across you, and treat
the answers as directional rather than exact. Each costs some volume while it
runs, which is usually cheaper than a year of scaling a channel that was taking
credit for demand you already had.

## Small numbers lie

At early volume a good day is noise. A conversion rate from six visitors is not a
rate, and two weeks of improvement can be chance. Acting on it produces confident
movement in arbitrary directions, and the confidence is the expensive part.

Decide thresholds before looking: a minimum number of events before any
comparison counts, trends read over weeks rather than days, and honesty that a
split test needs far more traffic than an early product has. Until volume
supports one, prefer large changes whose effect would be obvious.

## A measurement plan that fits on one page

The plan is written before anything is instrumented, because instrumentation
built without one produces events nobody can interpret a quarter later.

Name the north-star, in one line, with the definition of what counts. "Paying
customers" needs to say whether a customer who cancelled in the trial counts, and
deciding that later means the history either side of the decision is not
comparable.

List the three or four inputs that feed it, each with an owner and a definition
of the same rigour.

For each input, write the decision it settles. If the sentence cannot be written,
the number is a cost rather than an instrument.

Write down the reporting rhythm and stick to it. A number looked at daily at
early volume is being read as noise; the same number read weekly is a trend.

Write down the thresholds before the first look: the minimum number of events
before any comparison counts, and the size of change that would be acted on.
Deciding these afterwards is how a chart that went up becomes a strategy.

## Counting the funnel as rates rather than totals

A total tells you what happened. A rate tells you where to work, and the rates
between adjacent steps are where the constraint is visible.

For each pair of adjacent steps, record how many arrived and how many continued.
Visitors to the page, and how many started the form. Started, and how many
finished. Registered, and how many took the first meaningful action. Activated,
and how many paid. Paid, and how many were still there after the period you care
about.

The step with the worst rate relative to what is normal for that step is the
constraint, and improving anything else moves the total less. This is the whole
value of measuring the funnel rather than the outcome: an outcome tells you that
the quarter was bad, and the rates tell you which afternoon of work would fix it.

Two cautions. Rates computed on small numbers are not rates, and the same
threshold discipline applies here as everywhere. And a rate can improve because
the traffic got smaller and better rather than because the step got better, which
is why the totals stay on the page beside them.

## Cohorts, because averages hide the trend

An average over everybody who ever signed up is dominated by history and moves
slowly, so it cannot tell you whether the changes you are making are working.

Group people by the period in which they arrived and follow each group forward.
Then the question becomes whether people who arrived this month behave better
than people who arrived three months ago, which is the question a change is
trying to answer.

The retention curve read this way is the single most informative chart an early
product has. A curve that falls and then flattens means a group of people found
lasting value, and the business has something to grow. A curve that falls to
nothing means acquisition is filling a bucket with a hole in it, and spending
more on acquisition makes the hole more expensive rather than less.

That distinction decides whether the plan should be about growth at all, and it
is worth establishing before any acquisition budget is committed.

## The payback calculation, and why the period matters

Two numbers decide whether acquisition is sustainable: what it costs to acquire a
customer, and how much margin that customer produces over a period you choose in
advance.

Choose the period from how long the business can wait for the money, not from how
long a customer might theoretically stay. A lifetime value computed over three
years is a forecast; the same figure over the next six months is a fact you can
plan against. Using the forecast to justify present spending is the most common
way a growth plan runs out of cash while every chart looks healthy.

Use margin rather than revenue. Revenue-based ratios ignore the cost of serving
the customer, and in businesses where that cost is significant they endorse
spending that loses money on every sale.

Worked through: a customer paying 20 units a month at 70 percent margin produces
14 units of margin a month. If the business can wait six months for payback, the
sustainable acquisition cost is about 84 units. If a channel is acquiring at 120,
it is not a channel to scale, whatever the annual projection says.

Recompute it when the price, the costs, or the retention curve move, and treat
the number as a ceiling rather than a target.

## When to run a holdout, and how to keep it readable

The approximations above are worth scheduling rather than reaching for when
something looks wrong, because a channel taking credit for demand you already had
looks identical to a channel working on every report either of you can produce.
By the time it looks wrong, the budget has already been committed for months.

Run one before scaling rather than after. A week of pause costs a week and
answers whether the total moves; discovering the same thing after six months of
scaling costs six months and the spend.

Change one thing at a time while it runs. Three initiatives starting in the same
week produce one result that none of them can claim, and the argument about who
earned it is decided by whoever has the most confident chart.

Decide the read before it starts: which total you are watching, over how long,
and what size of movement counts. A holdout judged afterwards is judged by
whoever wanted the answer.

Expect the honest outcome to be uncomfortable at least once. The point of the
exercise is that it can say a channel you like is buying customers you already
had, and a test that cannot return that answer is not a test.

## How this gets misread

**Reading a platform's own report as the truth about the business.** Every
advertising platform reports conversions it believes it influenced, which
includes people who would have converted anyway. Comparing two platforms' reports
to each other compares two different questions.

**Rate improvements caused by falling volume.** A conversion rate that rises while
the total falls is usually the traffic changing rather than the page improving.

**A good week read as a trend.** At early volume the variance between weeks is
larger than most real improvements, and the confident version of this error is
the expensive one.

**Attribution changed mid-quarter.** Any change to how conversions are counted
severs the comparison across the change, and the change is often made by a
provider without notice. Record when it happens, in the same place the numbers
live.

**A number nobody owns.** When it moves, three people have an explanation and
none of them is accountable, so nothing is done.

## When to bring in a human

When tracking has to gate real spending, because quietly broken measurement is
worse than none and looks identical to the working kind. When the data touches
regulated categories or personal information, where the rules are not a marketing
judgement. And when the disagreement between sources is large enough to change
the budget, which is where somebody who does this for a living pays for
themselves in an afternoon.
