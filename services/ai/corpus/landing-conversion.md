# Landing pages and conversion

## Message match is the first thing to check

The page has to continue the sentence the ad or the link started. When someone
clicks a specific promise and lands on a generic homepage, they have to work out
whether they are in the right place, and most will not. The headline should
echo the promise that earned the click, in recognisably the same words.

This is the cheapest conversion fix available and the one most often skipped,
because the ad and the page are usually made at different times by different
people.

## One page, one action

Every additional option lowers the chance that any one of them is taken. A page
built for a campaign should ask for a single thing, and navigation that invites
the visitor to wander is working against it.

That single thing goes by a different name in every business: a signup, a
registration, a booked call, an enquiry, an app install, a completed purchase, a
place on a waiting list. The name matters far less than the discipline of picking
one and letting the whole page serve it. Pages that ask for two conversions
usually get neither, because the visitor reads the second option as evidence that
the first was not really the point.

Secondary information is not banned, it is subordinated. It appears below the
decision for the minority who want it, not alongside it competing for attention.

## The top of the page answers three questions

What this is, who it is for, and what happens next. A visitor who cannot answer
all three within a few seconds leaves, and no amount of persuasive material
further down is read.

"Who it is for" is the one most often omitted, and it is what makes the right
visitor feel recognised and lets the wrong one leave without costing anything.

## Specific proof beats adjectives

Claims about quality are discounted automatically because everyone makes them.
Evidence is not: a concrete number, a named use case, a before-and-after, a
quote that mentions the specific objection the reader is having.

Proof placed next to the point it supports works better than proof collected in
a testimonials section nobody scrolls to.

## Every field on the form costs conversions

Each additional input reduces completion, and the reduction is steeper on
phones. Ask only for what is needed to deliver the next step. Information that
would be useful later can be collected later, once the person has a reason to
give it.

Fields that feel invasive relative to what is being offered cost more than their
count suggests.

A pre-launch waitlist page is the clearest case of this. It can ask for an email
address and nothing else, because it is buying permission to talk later rather
than qualifying anybody now, and every extra question trades registrations for
information you have no use for yet.

## Speed and mobile are conversion features

A page that loads slowly on a phone on a normal connection loses visitors before
it renders, and paid traffic is disproportionately mobile. This is not a
technical nicety, it is a direct multiplier on every other improvement made to
the page.

## A/B testing at low traffic is mostly noise

Split tests need substantial volume to distinguish a real effect from chance. At
early-stage traffic levels, most declared winners are randomness, and acting on
them produces confident movement in arbitrary directions.

Until volume supports it, make larger changes rather than smaller ones, and take
direction from qualitative evidence: recordings of real sessions, the questions
people ask before buying, and where they stop reading. Small refinements are
worth running only once the traffic can prove them.

## The page in order

A campaign page has a sequence, and each section has one job. Sections that do
not have a job are what make a page long without making it convince.

**The headline** continues the promise that earned the click, in recognisably the
same words. It states the outcome, not the category.

**The subhead** says who it is for and what they are doing today. This is the
line that lets the wrong visitor leave without cost, which is a feature.

**The action**, visible without scrolling. A visitor who has already decided
should not have to hunt, and a visible action also tells the undecided what they
are being asked for, which is part of how they decide.

**The proof**, immediately after the claim it supports rather than gathered in a
section further down. One specific piece of evidence next to each claim beats a
wall of logos.

**How it works**, in three or four steps. Its job is not to teach the product, it
is to make the next step feel small and predictable.

**The objection**, answered directly. Whatever people ask before buying is what
goes here, in their words. This is the section most often missing and the one
that most often decides.

**The action again**, at the end, because the reader who has now read everything
should not have to scroll back.

Anything else on the page is being asked to justify its presence against the fact
that every element competes for the same attention.

## Writing the headline from the ad

Message match is easy to agree with and hard to actually do, so it helps to make
it mechanical.

Take the exact promise in the ad that earned the click. Write it as a sentence
about the reader's outcome. That sentence is the headline, adjusted only enough
to read naturally on a page rather than in a feed.

The test is to read the ad and the headline consecutively. If they sound like two
different products, or if the headline is broader than the ad, the visitor has to
work out whether they are in the right place, and most will not.

Where several ads point at one page, either the page needs a headline general
enough to be true of all of them, which weakens all of them, or it needs several
pages. Several pages is usually right, and is cheaper than it sounds, because
only the top of the page changes.

## The form

Ask only for what the next step actually needs. Everything else can be collected
once the person has a reason to give it, which is after they have received
something.

Order the fields from least to most invasive. A form that opens with a phone
number is abandoned before the visitor discovers the rest of it was easy.

Say what happens next, next to the button. "We will email the guide" or "You will
pick a time on the next screen" removes the specific hesitation of not knowing
what is about to happen.

Label the button with the outcome rather than the mechanism. "Get the guide" is a
sentence about the reader; "Submit" is a sentence about the database.

If the form must be long, split it so the easy part comes first. Someone who has
begun is far more likely to finish than someone looking at the whole thing at
once.

## When the page converts and the leads are bad

This is a different problem from low conversion, and treating it as a conversion
problem makes it worse.

Volume of the wrong people usually means the promise is broader than the product.
The page is attracting anyone with an adjacent problem, and the fix is to narrow
the headline and add the "who it is for" line, both of which will lower the
conversion rate and raise the value of what converts.

It can also mean the form is too easy for what is being asked. A page offering a
sales call with a single email field collects people who wanted a document. In
that case one qualifying question, chosen carefully, costs volume and buys
relevance.

Decide which you are optimising before changing anything, because the two moves
point in opposite directions and both look like improvements on their own chart.

## Finding where the page loses people without a testing tool

Split testing needs volume the page does not have. These do not.

Watch a few recorded sessions end to end. Ten is usually enough to see the same
hesitation three times, and what people do is more informative than what a
funnel chart says they did.

Read the questions arriving by email and chat. Every question asked before buying
is a section the page did not write.

Give the page to someone who has never seen it and ask them, after five seconds,
what it is and who it is for. Failing that test is the most common reason a page
underperforms, and no amount of further scrolling repairs it.

Check it on a phone, on a slow connection, cold, with the cache cleared. Paid
traffic is disproportionately mobile, and the page the founder sees on a fast
laptop is not the page the buyer sees.

## The step after conversion is part of conversion

The moment after someone signs up is when their intent is highest and it is
routinely wasted on a page that says "thank you".

Use it to deliver whatever was promised immediately, to state clearly what
happens next and when, and to ask for the one additional thing that is now
reasonable to ask, since the person has already committed once.

For a booked call, this is where the call gets confirmed and prepared. For a
waiting list, this is where the person is told when they will hear from you. For
a purchase, this is where the first useful action in the product is offered.

A registration that produces nothing for three days converts a warm person into
a cold one before anybody has spoken to them.

## When the page is not the problem

Some pages underperform for reasons a page cannot fix, and continuing to edit
them is expensive.

If the traffic arrived on a promise the product does not keep, the page is
telling the truth and the ad is not.

If the offer asks a stranger for a large commitment, no headline makes that
decision smaller.

If the visitors do not have the problem, nothing on the page is relevant to them,
and the fix is upstream in targeting or positioning.

The tell for all three is that competent, genuinely different versions of the
page all perform about the same. Pages differ from each other when the page is
the constraint. When they do not, something before the page is.
