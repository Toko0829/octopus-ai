# Influencer and partnership outreach

## What a partnership actually buys

A partnership puts the product in front of an audience somebody else has already
earned, carried by a recommendation from the person that audience trusts. The
endorsement is the asset. Nothing else in the funnel produces it.

The trade is control. You brief a partner and they decide how to say it, which is
uncomfortable and is precisely the reason it works: an audience that wanted your
wording would have read your own page.

## Choose by audience fit, not follower count

A creator with two thousand subscribers who are exactly your buyers outperforms
one with two hundred thousand who are not, at a fraction of the cost. Reach among
the wrong people flatters a report and produces nothing.

Ask who is actually in this audience, whether they act on recommendations or only
watch, and whether anything adjacent has worked here before. Comments are more
informative than follower counts, because they show whether anybody is listening.
All of this depends on knowing who your buyers are; if that is vague, the work
belongs upstream in positioning rather than here.

## Building the list of partners to approach

The list is the input to everything else, and a short list of well-chosen names
outperforms a long one assembled from a directory.

Start where your buyers already are. The people whose work your customers mention
in conversation, the newsletters they read, the communities they participate in,
the podcasts they listen to. Asking a handful of existing customers what they
read is faster and more accurate than any discovery tool.

Then look at who the people you already found reference. Creators in a niche
quote and appear alongside each other, and the second layer is usually less
approached than the first.

Then look at who has covered adjacent products. Somebody who has done a
partnership before knows how they work and will not need the concept explained.

For each name, record one specific thing about their work and one reason their
audience in particular is yours. If neither can be written, the name does not
belong on the list, because the outreach will read as generic and it will be.

Keep it in a plain list with the state of each conversation. Partnership outreach
is a pipeline, and the most common failure is not rejection but losing track of
the people who said not yet.

## What to pay a creator, and the shapes a partnership deal takes

Four shapes cover almost every creator partnership: a flat fee for agreed
content, a commission on the sales or leads that result, free product with no
fee, or a hybrid of a modest fee plus commission that splits the risk.

Anchor the number to what the outcome is plausibly worth, not to follower count,
because follower count is what the other side will anchor to. Estimate the
signups, bookings or purchases one collaboration could realistically drive,
multiply by what one is worth to you, and let that set the ceiling. If a
collaboration plausibly drives twenty signups and a signup is worth five units,
the deal is worth about a hundred, whatever the audience size suggests.

Start smaller than feels ambitious. One paid test with a clear brief tells you
whether this partner converts; partners who convert can be scaled, and partners
who do not have cost one fee rather than a quarter's budget. Expect most to
produce very little. It is a portfolio, not a bet. Recurring arrangements with
the few who work outperform a constant search for new ones, because a second
mention from a trusted person converts better than the first.

## Negotiating without an anchor you did not choose

The other side will anchor on audience size, because that is the number they have
and it is what every other approach has paid on. Your anchor is the ceiling
computed above, worked out before the conversation starts.

Bring the estimate rather than the budget. Saying you value the collaboration at
around a hundred, because that is what the signups it should produce are worth,
is a position. Asking what they charge hands the anchor over.

Where the rate is above your number, the honest options are a smaller scope, a
hybrid with a commission component, or walking away. Paying above the ceiling
because the audience is impressive is how a quarter's budget goes on one post.

Where a partner will not take commission, that is information rather than an
insult. Commission-only deals ask the partner to carry the risk of your
conversion rate, which they cannot assess, and established creators decline them
for that reason.

Agree the deliverable precisely: what is made, where it appears, when, and how
long it stays up. Vagueness here is what produces the dispute later, and it is
almost always about how long rather than about money.

## The outreach itself

Short, specific, and evidently written by somebody who has seen their work. Say
what you are, why their audience in particular, and what you propose, in that
order and in a few sentences. Ask for something small first.

Template blasts are deleted because they read as exactly what they are, and the
people worth working with get several a day. Expect a low reply rate even when
this is done well: it is a pipeline of leads like any other, and the arithmetic
works only if the list is long enough and the follow-up is polite, once.

In practice that means four things. Reference something specific they made and
say what about it was useful, which is the entire difference between a message
that gets read and one deleted in the first second, and which cannot be faked at
scale. Propose something concrete: asking whether they are open to a paid post is
answerable, while asking whether they would like to explore a partnership
requires the recipient to design the deal, which is work you are asking a
stranger to do for free. Say that it is paid in the first message, because
withholding it to start a conversation wastes the time of people whose rates are
above your budget and they will remember it. And follow up exactly once, after a
reasonable gap, adding something rather than repeating, because the difference
between persistence and nuisance here is one message.

## Running the collaboration

Give a brief, not a script. One page: the single message that must land, the
claims that must not be made, and the practical details. Everything else is
theirs, because a creator reading your copy aloud sounds like a script, which is
the one thing you are paying to avoid.

Give every partner their own link or code. Without it you cannot tell which
partnership produced the enquiries and installs, and you will renew whoever came
most recently to mind rather than whoever worked. Agree in advance whether you
may reuse the material yourself, and for how long.

## Measuring a partnership honestly

Record three numbers per collaboration: what it cost, what its own link produced,
and what the total for the period did. The third is there because tracked results
undercount. People see a recommendation, do not click, and arrive later by search
or directly, so a partnership can look like a failure on its own link and still
be visible in the week's total.

Ask new customers how they heard about you. That is where partnership effects
show up when the link does not catch them.

Judge a partner on a repeat rather than on one post. The first mention introduces
you and the second converts, which means single tests systematically undervalue
partners who would have worked.

Keep the results in one place across partners. The pattern in which kinds of
audience convert is worth more after five collaborations than any single result,
and it feeds targeting and positioning rather than only the next partnership.

## Failure shapes

**Paying for reach among the wrong people.** The report looks impressive and
nothing arrives. Almost always caused by choosing on audience size.

**Scripting the creator.** The material sounds like an advertisement because it
is one, and the audience discounts it exactly as they discount advertising. The
endorsement was the thing being bought, and it was spent.

**One large bet.** A single expensive collaboration has nothing to compare
against and cannot be learned from. Several small ones cost the same and produce
a ranking.

**No tracking agreed in advance.** Retrofitting attribution after publication is
not possible, and the collaboration becomes an anecdote.

**Discovering disclosure obligations afterwards.** Both sides carry an obligation
here, and it belongs in the brief rather than in a conversation after the post is
already live.

## Disclosure is not optional

Paid and gifted partnerships are advertising, and the relationship must be
disclosed clearly wherever the recommendation appears. That is an obligation on
both sides rather than a courtesy, and a platform's own partnership label may not
be sufficient by itself. The specifics are covered separately; the rule here is
that it belongs in the brief rather than being discovered afterwards.

## When to bring in a human

Contracts carrying usage rights, exclusivity or a term of months are negotiation
and legal territory rather than marketing judgement, as is any partner dispute
once it has started. The outreach and the brief can be drafted for you. The
signature and the terms should not be.
